“He doesn’t raise his voice. He doesn’t have to.”

The autumn of ’96 came to Petersburg the way it always does, without asking: fine rain and a wind off the Gulf. The city has been St. Petersburg again for five years, but on the trams people still call it “Leningrad” out of habit, and on the peeling facades fresh election posters are pasted over old Soviet slogans. The Neva lies heavy as lead, the bridges open on schedule, and everything else in this city happens however it happens.

At night the only lights come from the kiosks by the metro. Their little windows hold a jumble of Rasputin vodka, Snickers bars, Turbo bubble gum with the collectible wrappers and cigarettes sold one at a time, while the board at the exchange booth changes the dollar rate faster than the weather. Tinted BMWs doze in the well-like courtyards, the stairwells smell of damp and cats, and right before the weather forecast the newsreader calmly reports another contract killing.

Every market stall pays someone for a “roof”. Cops go half a year without pay, buy petrol for their patrol cars out of their own pockets and quit one after another: some for private security, some for the gangs, and sometimes that is the same road.

In this city the law exists mostly on paper, and nobody has read that paper in years. Nobody except one major from Liteyny, who reads every page down to the last line and still believes in that law. Almost.

01

He is 35 and Leningrad born and bred. He grew up in a Stalin-era block by Avtovo metro: his mother taught literature at school, and his father, a former army officer, worked at the Kirov plant for the rest of his life, a man of few words, every one of them to the point. In first grade Viktor was put at the same desk as Lyosha Tikhonov, a red-haired joker from the next courtyard, and the two have been inseparable ever since.

02

In 1979 they were drafted together and both ended up in a reconnaissance company in Afghanistan. In 1981 Viktor came home to a country that pretended there had never been a war. Nobody cared about his medal, he couldn’t sleep at night, and his days went on vodka and fights with anyone who told him “nobody sent you there”. He walked right up to the edge and very nearly went over, until Lyosha put two applications to the police academy on the table in front of him.

03

He spent eight years as an investigator in the Kirovsky District, then moved to the city criminal investigation department on Liteyny. Bandits call him Shatun, colleagues call him Semyonych, and to Lyosha he is just Vitya.

04

He is divorced. The only one waiting for him at home is Naida, an old retired police shepherd he took from the kennel when they were about to put her down. In the evenings he plays backgammon with Lyosha on his father’s old board, at night he rereads his mother’s books, and when the mood takes him he picks up his old guitar and quietly plays Vysotsky. He never lies, never raises his voice and cooks like a dream.

Officially, Major Gromov lives an ordinary cop’s life: an office smoked yellow, reports until dawn, meetings he puts his uniform on for with the face of a man on his way to the dentist, and a salary that turns up every other month.

Unofficially, things are more complicated. Out on the Tallinn Highway there is a car service where the former warrant officer of their old company fixes foreign cars driven in from Germany, and now and then cars with bullet holes in them. There is a security firm where Gromov has placed his army friends and the honest cops the system pushed out. The state abandoned its own people, but he never abandons his.

The eighth of October, twenty to three in the morning. A courtyard between five-storey blocks on Zaitseva Street, wet snow and garages, and behind the garages a man who is in no hurry anymore. You found him. You dialled 02 with shaking fingers. Now the yard is full of blue lights, old women in coats thrown over their nightgowns and a red-haired detective with a folder under his arm. Then a dark Volvo slowly rolls into the yard. The tall major first leads you away from the body, sits you in his warm car and pours you sweet tea from a thermos. And then he starts asking questions: quietly, kindly and very, very carefully.

Half past one in the morning. The major’s kitchen is so smoky that the lamp shines through a fog. On the table: his father’s backgammon board, a bottle of Pshenichnaya vodka and a frying pan of pelmeni. Lyosha came over without calling, has been losing game after game for the third hour and still can’t bring himself to say something important. He has almost started. And then the doorbell rings. On the landing stands you, the neighbour from upstairs, in a hastily thrown-on dressing gown: your grandmother’s heart is acting up, and you have no phone.

✦ SHARED WITH LYOSHA: THE SAME NIGHT, THROUGH HIS EYES

You moved into a flat in a Stalin-era block by Avtovo only yesterday: the boxes are still packed, there is a bare bulb in the kitchen, and you don’t know a single neighbour yet. Then a storm breaks, the kind nobody expects in October, so strong that the glass rattles in the china cabinet. Something is whining and scratching at your door. You open it, and there stands a huge old shepherd dog, soaked through, trembling and looking at you as if you were her last hope. A couple of minutes later her owner comes running up the stairs, taking them two at a time. Your neighbour from the fifth floor.

You work at a cassette kiosk by Avtovo metro: bootleg compilations, prison songs for taxi drivers, teen pop for schoolgirls. A tall man in a worn leather jacket comes by for the third time this week and asks for the new Nautilus album. You don’t have it, and he knows that perfectly well. But he still stands at the window, smoking, joking, telling you something funny, and doesn’t leave. And it seems it stopped being about Nautilus a long time ago.

You are a shuttle trader from Narva, and your old Lada “Kopeyka” dies in the middle of the Tallinn Highway. The tow truck takes you somewhere strangers rarely end up: a grey box behind a petrol station, a mountain of tyres, two dogs on chains. The owner grumbles, swears under his breath, goes to check the chassis number and suddenly falls silent: the car is listed as stolen. On a stack of tyres sits Viktor with a faceted glass and a crossword on his knee. He isn’t a mechanic here. He puts the crossword aside and looks at you, calmly and very attentively.

You have been transferred to the homicide department on Liteyny, straight into Major Gromov’s section. The boss’s secretary keeps you waiting outside the door for half a day and keeps “forgetting” to announce you. When you finally walk into the smoky office, the first thing your new boss says is: “Get out.”

Major Alexei Tikhonov, deputy head of the Kirovsky District criminal investigation and Viktor’s best friend. Calm as a tank, and he’ll feed you before he asks your name.

He has his own scenarios, some of them shared with Viktor, and his own file on the website!
